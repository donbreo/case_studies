To play the video you're watching now , YouTube created special hardware. They designed the chip
specifically for video processing because, on the scale of YouTube, general-purpose processors were
no longer the most efficient way to get the job done. And this is just one of the unusual
engineering solutions hidden behind this platform. And this is due to the scale at which YouTube
operates. Every day, viewers watch approximately one billion hours of video on YouTube. That's about
5 billion unique views for over 2 billion users in almost every country in the world. At the same
time, creators upload about 500 hours of new video every minute. Over 8 hours of video is coming in
every second. And while you're watching this video, thousands more hours of content will be
uploaded. And each of these videos must be processed, stored, indexed, replicated, and prepared for
broadcast across a vast array of devices and network conditions. And all of this raises a simple
question. How does YouTube actually work ? But the answer to this is not so simple. Most engineers
assume that the most difficult thing is delivering a billion hours of video every day. This is
certainly impressive, but that's not where the main complexity lies. The most difficult problem
begins much earlier, when the creator clicks " download". Before a video can be streamed, YouTube
must convert it into dozens of different formats, resolutions, and bitrates, distribute it
worldwide, and make it instantly searchable by billions of users. A significant portion of
engineering efforts are directed toward solving this problem. And in this video, we'll break down
the entire architecture: from loading and processing to storage, databases, and global delivery. And
everything in this video is taken from published Google articles, engineering reports, and technical
documentation. I turned all the publicly available information into these visual diagrams. So this
video is going to be very visual. Stay until the end to understand how one of the greatest systems
ever created really works. YouTube does a huge amount of work only once, and that happens the moment
a video is uploaded . Therefore, he can do almost nothing. billions of times when people press the "
play" button. And that's the whole strategy. All the hard work is done upfront, and by the time you
click on the video, the bytes you're about to watch have already been processed into dozens of
versions and are already on a server a few miles from your house. Pressing the “play” button is
actually cheap precisely because all the expensive processing has already been done. So, the
principle here is simple. Calculate once and broadcast forever. And every design decision we'll look
at today is aimed at making the most common use case—pressing the "play" button —as cheap as
possible. So let's start with where the work actually begins - with the download. When a creator
uploads a video, they upload one file, one resolution, one format—what their camera or editing
software produced. This single file should be available for everyone to view. Here's someone
watching on a 4K TV over gigabit fiber internet. Someone else is on a six-year-old Android phone
with an unstable 3G connection somewhere in the village. Someone is watching from a laptop, and
someone, perhaps even from a " smart" refrigerator. So each of these people has their own screen
size, different bandwidth, and their own set of video formats that their device can decode.
Therefore, you can't just save one file provided by the author and distribute it to everyone. A 4K
file will never download on your phone. It will buffer endlessly, use up all your mobile data, and
the small, low-quality file will look terrible on a big TV. So, one file cannot satisfy everyone.
And therein lies the main problem. One input load and an almost infinite variety of devices and
networks at the output. Bridging this gap is the most expensive part of the job. So let's follow the
path of the video. So, the author clicks the " download" button. The first thing that happens is
that the video is not sent as one huge object. A program on the user's side breaks the file into
smaller pieces , typically 10–50 megabytes each, and downloads them. And there is a very practical
reason for this. Downloads may be interrupted. Your Wi-Fi may disappear. You can get into an
elevator and your phone will switch from Wi-Fi to mobile data . If you were downloading one huge
file and it stopped at 90%, you would have to start all over again, which would be very annoying. By
breaking the file into pieces, YouTube only has to re-upload the part that didn't make it through,
leaving everything that has already been transferred in place. This is what people mean when they
talk about "resumable booting." A download that can continue from where it left off. This is why
YouTube uploads can withstand an unstable connection that would kill a single large data transfer.
These fragments are loaded to the nearest Google edge point—the closest entry point to the Google
network. So data doesn't have to travel a long distance to be safe inside their system. They are put
together, and the complete original file goes into storage. After that, YouTube adds a message in
turn, which effectively announces: "here's a new video." This needs processing, so a pool of worker
machines monitors this queue, and one of them takes on the task. And now the most expensive part
begins. So, you remember the problem. Every device, any bandwidth. YouTube takes the original and
transcodes it into a whole gamut of resolutions. If someone uploads a video in 8K, YouTube creates
about nine different versions of the resolution . 144p for the worst connections, then 240, 360,
480p, 720p, 1080, 1440, 2160, which is 4K, and all the way up to 4320p, which is full 8K, and each
of those is a separate video file created from that same upload. But it doesn't end with
resolutions. Devices also differ in their opinions regarding the format, or codec—the compression
scheme used to compress the video. Older devices only understand the old H.264 codec, newer ones can
work with more modern and efficient codecs like VP9, ​​and the newest ones support an even better
one—AV1. So YouTube doesn't just create nine resolution options . It makes several such resolutions
in different codecs so that any device can find a version that it can play. If you do the math, that
's about 9 resolutions multiplied by multiple codecs plus separate audio tracks, and one download
turns into dozens of separate files. The process of converting a video file from one format to all
others is called transcoding, and it is actually very expensive. It's actually shockingly expensive.
It's so expensive that it affects everything YouTube builds next. Transcoding, as I mentioned, is an
extremely difficult task, and the reason lies in one compromise built into the way codecs work. The
main goal of a codec is to make a video file as small as possible while maintaining quality, as
smaller files mean less bandwidth, which means faster downloads and lower costs. But that's the
catch. The better a codec compresses a file, the more computing power it requires for this
compression. So, the smarter the compression, the more complex the mathematical calculations and the
more CPU time you burn. The modern VP9 codec compresses much better than the older H.264, but it
requires about five times more computation to encode. And AV1, the newest, compresses even better
than VP9. It can reduce the file size by another 30% or so, but it is even more computationally
expensive to create. So the formats that save the most bandwidth are precisely those that require
the most capacity to create them. Now put that on the scale. 8 hours of new video every second. Each
of them requires all these versions and all these expensive codecs. This is one of the largest
computational tasks on the entire planet. But YouTube approached it from two completely different
angles. And the first one concerns how you organize your work . Here is the first clever trick.
Let's say someone downloads a 2- hour movie. If you were to process it the obvious way— on a single
machine from start to finish, frame by frame—it would take forever. You would process it at a speed
close to playback, and a 2- hour video would take a long time to load on the machine. That's why
YouTube doesn't do that. It breaks the video into many short segments. Imagine cutting a 2- hour
movie into a bunch of 10-minute pieces. It hands each piece to another machine, and they all process
their part at the same time, in parallel, using a huge fleet of executors. So, instead of one
machine " chewing" 2 hours of video, you have 12 machines, each processing 10 minutes at a time.
When they finish, the system stitches the processed parts back together in queue order into one
finished video. So work that would take hours is now done in a fraction of that time. But there's
one subtle problem this creates: when you cut a video at the boundaries of fragments, the machine
processing the part after the cut doesn't see the frames before the cut. And for high-quality video
compression, it is critically important to see neighboring frames. Therefore, small quality defects
may occur precisely at the joints where fragments meet. The solution is to allow the fragments to
overlap slightly. Each fragment includes several frames from neighboring parts on both sides. This
way the encoder gets the necessary context. And these extra frames are cut off when everything is
stitched together. So, parallelism turns a task that would take hours into something that can be
done quickly. But there is a limit to how far you can go by simply dividing the work. And this is
where YouTube took radical action. Okay, parallelism allows you to achieve a lot. But around 2015,
YouTube analyzed the trends: more downloads, higher resolutions, and all these new codecs that
require five times more computing power. And they realized that simply increasing the number of
regular servers would not help scale the system . Conventional processors are universal. They are
designed to do a little bit of everything , which means they are not specifically adapted for one
thing. And video transcoding is a very specific thing. So they did something most companies would
never dare to do. They developed their own special chip. A silicon crystal designed to perform only
one task: video transcoding , and nothing more. They called it the Argos VCU, a video encoding
device. And because it's built specifically for this one task, it's significantly more efficient
than a regular processor. We are talking about computational efficiency that is 20–23 times higher
when considering the full cost of design, creation, and training. According to one analysis, these
chips performed work that would otherwise have taken about 10 million processors . Each chip has 10
dedicated encoding cores, and each core can process 4K video in real time. They place two such chips
on a card that looks a lot like a graphics card, and then fill data centers with them. When you
download a video today, it's almost certainly being processed by one of these special chips, which
is the main reason why 4K video is ready in hours instead of days like it used to be. And if you
watched my video about Dropbox's system design , this should look familiar to you, because Dropbox
builds its own storage hardware because they understood their workload deeply enough to specialize.

Okay, back to the video. We've dealt with the video files themselves, but video is not just pixels.
Think about everything else that goes with it . The title, description , the channel it belongs to,
the ever-growing number of views, likes, every comment and every reply to it, which videos are in
which playlist. Your subscriptions, viewing history, watch later list—all of this is structured
data, strings, and relationships. This is exactly what traditional relational databases are ideally
suited for. And the relational database that YouTube turned to long ago is one you already know.
MySQL, plain, boring, battle-tested MySQL. And if you've seen my video about Instagram's system
design, you know exactly where this is going. Instagram scaled to hundreds of millions of users on
Postgres, a boring relational database that everyone said didn't scale. YouTube did the same with
MySQL. At least in the beginning, and none of them escaped to some "fashionable" databases. Then it
got difficult. They stayed and figured out how to make a boring, well- understood tool work at large
scales. So let's see what's broken and what they've built to fix it. One MySQL database works
perfectly fine for a while, and then problems arise. The same thing happened with YouTube. The first
problem is connectivity. Every time an application opens a connection to a database, it must
allocate a portion of memory just to manage that connection. And when you have a huge fleet of
application servers that are constantly opening connections, you can get thousands and thousands of
these connections. And the database wastes gigabytes of memory just maintaining open connections
before performing even a single useful action. The second problem is the limitation of one machine.
At some point, your data and traffic simply become bigger than the largest server you can buy , and
there's nowhere left to grow. Third is the pain of sharding. The classic answer to the limitation is
to distribute data across multiple database servers, i.e. sharding. But if your application code
needs to know which server contains which data, your code becomes confusing, fragile, and scary to
change. And the fourth is fault tolerance. When a database server goes down , someone has to notice
it, bring up a backup server, and redirect traffic . And all this should happen quickly, without
data loss and without calls to the programmer at 3 am . These are the same problems that any
database of this scale faces. So , YouTube hasn't abandoned MySQL. They created a layer on top of it
that absorbs all that complexity. And this level is called Vitess. Vitess is a system written in
Golang that sits between your application and MySQL servers, making an entire fleet of MySQL
databases behave as one single database. Your application “thinks” that it is accessing one regular
MySQL server, but behind the scenes, Vitess manages dozens or hundreds of such servers. It all
started inside YouTube. It handled all of YouTube's database traffic for years, and is now an open-
source project used by large companies like Slack, Square, GitHub, and others. It consists of two
main parts, and it is worth knowing their names. The first is VTGate. This is the router, the "
front door". Your application connects to VTGate as if it were a regular database. And when a
request comes in, VTGate first determines which MySQL server or servers can respond to it and
redirects it there. Your application doesn't need to know exactly how the data is partitioned.
VTGate hides all of this. The second part is VTTablet, and such an element stands in front of each
MySQL server, next to it, on the same machine. It's like a little security guard for each database.
Let me show you exactly what it does, because three of its functions are worth looking at in more
detail. So its first function is connection pooling, and that's a direct solution to that first
obstacle. Instead of letting thousands of application connections "hit" directly into MySQL and
consume all the memory , VTTablet maintains a small pool of real database connections, say around
100, and multiplexes all incoming traffic into this pool. So you can have 10,000 connections from
your applications, and they all go to about 100 real connections to MySQL. Database memory is freed
up to do real work, instead of just keeping connections open. Connection pooling is one of the first
things that really saves a relational database when scaling. And its second function is query
security. In a huge codebase with hundreds of engineers, someone will eventually write a bad query.
For example, a query that forgot to limit the number of rows and tries to retrieve 10 million
records, " crashing" the entire database. So, Vitess Tablet checks requests before executing them
and can intervene, set a limit, block a known malicious request, or abort one that is running too
long and exhausting resources. So it's like a seatbelt for people who, with good intentions, make
costly mistakes. And the third task is deduplication. This is a little complicated, so hear me out.
Imagine an extremely popular video whose view count is read by thousands of people simultaneously,
without any tricks. These are thousands of identical read requests that literally " bombard" the
database for the same number. So here's what Vitess does. When a request comes in to read something
that is already being received, it does not launch a second identical request. It simply makes the
new request wait for the one that is already executing. And when the answer comes, everyone who was
waiting gets the result at the same time. In this way, a thousand identical requests are collapsed
into one real one. So: pooling, query safety, and duplicate read reduction. This Vitess Tablet does
its quiet work. But Vitess has another task, and it's the most difficult of all. There is one more
thing that Vitess does, and this is the most difficult part for any database at scale: if one shard,
i.e. a piece of data, becomes too large or overloaded, it needs to be split into smaller shards to
distribute the load. And the horror is doing it while the product is running, with people constantly
reading and writing, without downtime and without losing a single row of data. Vitess automates
this. It creates new shards, copies the data, keeping it in sync with current changes, and when
everything is verified to match, switches reads to the new shards first. Only after it has been
proven safe does he switch the recording. If at any stage something looks wrong, you can roll back
and the data will continue to flow all the time. YouTube users don't notice anything at all . And if
you look closely, it's actually the same disciplined migration pattern from my Dropbox video, where
you copy the data, run both systems in parallel, check their consistency, and then carefully switch
reads and then writes. Safe migration is never about tricks. It's about patience and taking each
stage one at a time. And as we saw in the Instagram video, you separate the logical data structure
from the physical hardware so you can scale without rewriting the code. So, Vitess is the same thing
, but automated. So, Vitess distributes MySQL across a huge number of machines, but there is a more
complex database problem that it does not solve: ensuring data integrity when it is dispersed across
the planet. YouTube data is stored in data centers on different continents. That is, a copy in the
USA, a copy in Europe, a copy in Asia. And for everyone to see the same thing, these copies must
match the order in which the changes occurred. Which entry was the first and which was the second?
It's easy on one machine. There is only one clock. You timestamp each change and sort by it. But on
a global scale, every car has its own clock. So no two clocks match, and they constantly diverge by
milliseconds. So when a recording appears in New York and another in Tokyo a little later, and you
compare the timestamps, you can't really trust which one came first. The clocks are too far apart to
determine this. And if you get the order wrong, the copies will become inconsistent, and someone in
one part of the world will see one thing and someone else will see something else. The usual way to
synchronize clocks over a network leaves an error of 100 milliseconds or more, which is not accurate
enough. So, on a planetary scale, the database can't even agree on what time it is. So Google solved
this with a database called Spanner. They stopped trusting regular watches. Google has installed
real atomic clocks and GPS receivers inside its data centers. The same atomic clock technology that
provides official world time is now found in server racks. Thanks to them, each machine knows the
exact time, with a deviation of a few milliseconds from any other machine on Earth. Then they
created an add-on called TrueTime. When the database asks what time it is, TrueTime doesn't return a
single number because a single number would imply precision that doesn't exist. It returns a tiny
interval. She says that real time is somewhere between this moment and this very moment. And thanks
to atomic clocks, this interval is only a few milliseconds. And now the key step: when the database
saves a record, it intentionally waits for this tiny interval to completely pass before considering
the record complete. This purposeful waiting lasts for a few milliseconds. And by waiting for the
period of uncertainty to end , it ensures that any subsequent record anywhere on the planet will
receive a later timestamp. No machine ever has to ask others to tell it what time it is. So they
trust the clock and wait. Thus, the entire planet coordinates the order of events, using time itself
as a coordinator. The price is the few milliseconds added to each write, and it's the cost of
maintaining data consistency around the world. And this is the same move as with the special chip
they created. Everyone else struggled with this problem programmatically, synchronizing clocks over
the network with additional approximations. Google decided that the best solution was to change the
hardware and install atomic clocks in the building. Other databases later copied this idea
programmatically, because almost no one else can put an atomic clock in a rack. When physics became
the limitation, Google decided to change physics. Now the last element remains: how to deliver the
video to you, the viewer. So, the finished video is stored in Google storage. If each of the 8
billion hours had to travel from a central data server to your phone, the traffic costs would be
huge and everything would run slower. Therefore, YouTube physically brings popular videos closer to
you in advance, even before your request. This is a CDN, a content delivery network. And Google's
version is called Google Global Cache. The idea is quite bold. Google creates caching servers and
places them on the networks of Internet service providers , i.e. your ISP. The company you pay for
your internet has Google servers on its premises , preloaded with popular videos in your area. These
cache servers are located in over 1,300 cities in over 200 countries. So when you stream a popular
video, the bytes usually don't cross a country or an ocean. They come from a machine a few miles
away from you, inside your provider's network. This is why a viral video plays instantly. By the
time you click on it, the copies are already next to the viewers. And the work of bringing the video
to you has been done in advance. There's a good reason why an ISP would want to have Google's
servers on-site. It all comes down to how providers pay for traffic. Getting data from outside their
own network costs them real money. Traffic that stays within the network is cheap. Therefore, when
Google places a cache of popular videos inside the provider's network, the provider stops constantly
loading the same videos from outside. He provides them locally for almost free. Google calls it
offloading, and both parties win. Google gets the video closer to the viewers, and the provider
reduces traffic costs. That's why these cache servers are located at providers all over the planet.
And it works quite well. They satisfy most requests locally with a success rate of around 98-99% for
popular videos. The mechanism is simple. You are requesting a video. You first access the YouTube
server. It checks your IP address, sees that your provider has a cache, and sends a copy to your own
network. And if the video is not yet in this cache, a cache miss occurs. It takes the video from a
larger, higher- level cache, serves it to you, and keeps a copy for the next user. Popular videos
are cached in advance, while rare videos are cached only when someone requests them . There is
another delivery detail that is especially important for mobile networks. YouTube actively uses
QUIC— Google's transport protocol, which later became the basis of HTTP/3. To understand why this is
useful, look at how the older TCP protocol behaves. TCP delivers data in strict order. If one packet
is lost, the recipient is forced to wait for it before it can use subsequent packets. The next
packages could have already arrived. It doesn't matter. Everything stops until a lost packet
appears. It's like one broken down car blocking an entire lane of traffic. This is called head-of-
line blocking. And this is especially painful in mobile networks, where packets are constantly lost.
Each lost packet means a short pause. If there are many, you see a loading icon. The QUIC protocol
was created precisely to avoid this. If a packet is lost, other data streams continue to flow while
the lost packet is retransmitted in the background. QUIC also establishes connections faster than
TCP, so videos start playing faster. The result is a much smoother viewing experience. Google
reported a significant reduction in re- buffering after switching to QUIC, especially on mobile
networks where connection quality constantly fluctuates. This is the kind of change that no one
notices directly. You just get fewer interruptions. Now let's take a broader look, because one
design principle runs through everything we've considered. YouTube spends a huge amount of effort
even before a video is viewed for the first time. The uploaded video needs to be processed,
converted to different formats and resolutions, optimized for different devices, and distributed to
caches around the world. All this work is expensive, but it is only done once. Instead, the review
process is designed to be as simple as possible. Therefore, when you click " play", the video is
usually served from the nearest cache. The network path is short, and the server infrastructure
performs a minimum of calculations. Expensive work is done once, while cheap work is done millions
of times. And almost every architectural solution we've seen on YouTube exists to support this
asymmetry. But there's another aspect: YouTube's library contains hundreds of millions of videos,
and most of them are watched very rarely. Many of them have only a few views, and some are hardly
ever watched. So it would be incredibly wasteful to fully optimize every video as if it were going
to be the next viral hit. Therefore, YouTube spends its most expensive resources precisely where
there is real demand. Popular videos receive wider distribution, more aggressive caching, and
additional optimization, while less frequently viewed content is treated much more conservatively.
So, there are five conclusions that can be drawn from this. The first is to do the expensive work in
advance. One, to keep the usual path cheap, YouTube does the most expensive part during the
download, so pressing the play button costs next to nothing, and the data is sent to the nearest
cache even before you ask for it. Second—boring tools work when scaled if you build a system around
them. YouTube processes its metadata on plain MySQL just as Instagram runs on Postgres. They didn't
switch to a trendy database. They built and tested to make boring tools scale. The third—things like
pooling, query safety, and running the job once—all of these concepts aren't very fancy, but they're
what hold everything together. Group your connections so that the database doesn't get choked up
with them. Stop bad requests before they are executed. Combine duplicate work instead of repeating
it. Fourth—when your problem is big and specific enough, owning a stack gives you advantages that
can't be bought. YouTube created a special chip for transcoding, and Google installed atomic clocks
in its data centers to maintain database consistency . Fifth—when the system is deployed across the
planet, the most difficult thing is to agree on the order in which events occurred. Google uses
atomic clocks and spends a few extra milliseconds on each entry just to keep events in order around
the world. Consistency on this scale is never free. You pay for it in hardware and latency, or you
give it up . If you enjoyed this video, I have two playlists worth your attention. There is a
