, satellite internet existed long before Starlink and operated on geostationary satellites, also
known as GEO. These are satellites orbiting at an altitude of 35,800 km above the Earth. At this
altitude, one revolution of the satellite around the Earth takes 24 hours , which means that it
constantly hangs over one point. From a network point of view, this is great because the satellite
is stationary in the sky, one device covers a third of the planet, and your antenna is rigidly fixed
at one point, and no switching between satellites is required during the entire operation . But
there is a big problem—the distance. It takes about 120 milliseconds for light to travel 36,000 km,
and your data packet goes up , comes back down, and the response does the same. That's about 500
milliseconds per complete cycle before the packet even hits the internet. When you add processing
and ground network delays, real-world GEO connections typically have around 600 milliseconds of
latency. This is acceptable for downloading files, but terrible for video communication, effectively
making them unsuitable for interactive things like video calls or gaming. Starlink's response was to
lower the altitude to 550 km, which is 65 times closer to Earth . At this altitude, the one-way
light travel time is less than 2 milliseconds, and the measured round-trip delays are between 20 and
40 milliseconds, most of which are due to the ground network. Due to the laws of orbital mechanics,
you cannot choose altitude and speed separately. At an altitude of 550 km, there is only one speed
option for maintaining orbit—it is about 7.5 km per second. If you fly slower, the satellite will
fall to Earth. So, while GEO satellites hang motionless in the sky due to their height, Starlink
satellites, due to their low orbit, cross the entire sky in a matter of minutes and very quickly
disappear over the horizon. Therefore, to ensure continuous user service, a chain of satellites is
needed, where each subsequent one picks up the work of the previous one. And to serve the entire
planet, thousands are needed. And it was precisely this engineering compromise that SpaceX chose.
Instead of a single fixed geostationary satellite, they created a low-earth orbit, or LEO, network
of 7,000 moving satellites, trading distance for movement and latency for complexity. And the first
engineering problem that all of this creates is the antenna on your roof. A traditional satellite
dish is a parabolic reflector on a motor. To track a Starlink satellite, the dish would have to
travel across the entire sky in 4 minutes, turn sharply, catch the next satellite, and do so
forever, in wind and cold, which would render it inoperable in a matter of months. So, it's not very
practical. If you are a pure software developer with no background in telecommunications or
electronics, the next part will be a bit difficult to understand, but I encourage you to listen
anyway, because it may help you in a few years when AI takes over software development and we all
have to work with "hardware ". In short, the Starlink terminal is not a dish at all. This is
actually a phased array antenna. These are hundreds of small antenna elements on a flat panel, each
transmitting the same signal with tiny individual time delays. If these time delays are set
correctly, signals from hundreds of antenna elements are combined into a narrow beam, and changing
the delay simply moves that beam. Try to imagine it this way. You electronically steer the beam
without moving the antenna itself. No motors or mechanical parts are involved in the tracking
process. This beam can be directed to any point in the sky in milliseconds, and all of this happens,
as I said, without motors, although the Starlink terminal does have a motor that is only used to
tilt the panel once during installation. Most importantly, the terminal or user dish never searches
for a satellite. They are actually being told where to look. The dish stores what's called an
ephemeris—a constantly updated record of where each satellite will be over time, along with its own
GPS coordinates. Based on these two data, calculating the angle at which the antenna should be
directed is just a mathematical problem. So, the position of the satellite in the sky is not
searched for, it is actually predicted. This same idea solves another problem, which, as one might
expect, is much more complex, namely the so-called Doppler effect. So, as a satellite approaches you
at 7.5 km/s, it compresses the radio waves it transmits, and this shifts their frequency by hundreds
of kHz in the Starlink operating bands. And as the satellite moves across the sky, this shift is
constantly changing, and this is the Doppler effect. So a conventional receiver would have to
measure and track this error in real time, but Starlink largely avoids this because since both the
satellite and the terminal know each other's predicted positions from the ephemeris, they can pre-
calculate the expected Doppler shift and compensate for it before transmission. And by the time the
signal reaches the receiver, most of the frequency error has already been eliminated. And this same
idea—prediction instead of detection—is not limited to the antenna. Starlink applies it to the
entire network. As it turns out, locking onto one satellite is the easy part, but deciding which
satellite should serve you for the next 15 seconds is much more difficult. So let's see how it goes
in the next section. Before we talk about how this is solved, let's take a look at how Starlink sees
the ground. For Starlink, the planet is divided into hexagonal cells, and if you look at Starlink's
own availability map, the cells it shows come from H3—an open-source geospatial indexing library
that Uber developed to divide the world into hexagons. So the same grid that can be used to group
ride requests is used to group satellite beams. Each satellite can only form a limited number of
narrow beams, and each beam covers approximately one hexagonal cell, and everyone within that cell
shares a common bandwidth resource . This means that Starlink is not selling the internet so much as
access to a beam over a certain area of ​​the Earth's surface. This is why some regions are marked
as sold out or with a waiting list, and it is not an artificial shortage . This means that every
beam that can serve this hexagon is already occupied, and adding another client would mean taking
capacity away from someone else. And this leads to a real planning problem. At any given moment,
thousands of satellites, each with a limited number of beams, move over millions of hexagonal cells
that require constant coverage. Every few seconds, the network has to decide which satellite should
cover which cell, and continue to make that decision while the entire array is moving overhead. So
who makes these decisions? Every 15 seconds, the entire group goes through a replanning phase. The
scheduler decides which satellite should cover which cells, calculates new assignments, and
distributes them throughout the network. Your terminal receives a simple instruction: stay on the
current satellite or switch to a new one. If switching is required, the beam moves in milliseconds.
This part was never published by the Starlink team, but the researchers figured it out on their own.
They installed special Starlink terminals, recorded the delay over a long period of time, and found
the same pattern. This delay changes in precise steps that occur at exactly 15 -second intervals,
synchronized for different users. In between these limits, the response time changes smoothly as
your serving satellite approaches or moves away. But at the border it changes abruptly. So the
entire network is actually replanned four times a minute, which is 5,760 times every day. The
planner doesn't just choose the nearest satellite. Every 15 seconds, it solves a global optimization
problem with dozens of constraints. The satellite must be at least 25° above your horizon. Below,
the signal passes through a too thick layer of the atmosphere at too sharp an angle, and each
satellite has a limited power reserve and a limited number of beams, so it can only serve a certain
number of cells at a time, and not every satellite can transmit your traffic to the Internet, since
the visibility of gateways and the licenses of specific countries determine which paths are legal in
general. So there is another restriction - regulatory. International spectrum usage rules define a
geostationary orbit exclusion zone to protect older geostationary satellites from interference.
Therefore, if a Starlink satellite moves into line of sight between your terminal and the
geostationary belt, it should stop transmitting to you before it reaches that zone. In fact, there
is an invisible no-transmission line moving across the sky— not because of physics, but because of
rules. The planner anticipates these intersections in advance and routes traffic around them, so
even regulation becomes part of the optimization problem. If you saw my video about how DataDog lost
40,000 servers in a minute, you know it was because all those servers changed at the same time, and
that's how the outage started. But in the case of Starlink, the entire network is intentionally
changing simultaneously four times a minute, and they still manage. The difference between them is
that for DataDog the changes were a surprise, while in Starlink they are calculated in advance, that
is, the future of the network is predicted and sent to all terminals as a schedule. Now let's
discuss an important issue. How does Starlink calculate or predict the future of its network?
Starlink can predict the future of the network because the future of the network is Newtonian. A
satellite in orbit does not move anywhere unpredictably. Its path is determined by gravity,
altitude, and a small correction for atmospheric drag. Knowing today's orbital elements, it is
possible to calculate where this satellite will be next Tuesday at 3:47 p.m., to within a few
meters. Every time of appearance over every hexagonal cell , every moment when any two satellites
will be in line of sight of each other, and every connection that will be geometrically possible
next week — all of this can be calculated right now. Compare this to the networks we use on Earth.
When a fiber optic cable is cut, a router fails, or someone uploads a misconfiguration, routing
protocols like OSPF start telling every other router which links are still active, while BGP
gradually enumerates new paths across the internet. And none of this can happen in advance because
ground faults are fundamentally unpredictable, and you can't plan for a construction crew to
accidentally cut a fiber optic cable. So, the whole job of our existing routing protocols, such as
OSPF and BGP, is to detect unexpected changes and respond to them as quickly as possible. And they,
in essence, do not predict, but react. And I hope this difference is quite clear. Currently, the
Starlink network topology is constantly changing, but unlike terrestrial networks, these changes are
almost entirely predictable. So Starlink turned this problem on its head. Instead of detecting the
network after it changes, it computes the network before it changes. The ground control center takes
the predicted orbits of each satellite, along with the location of each gateway and user cell, as
well as a full set of operational constraints , and then calculates the assignment for the next
planning interval. This plan is distributed to the satellite group before it becomes operational.
The data plane is in orbit and is the part of the network that directly forwards packets, while the
control plane remains on the ground. This is the part that decides where these packets should go.
And because the scheduler already knows where each satellite will be for the next 15 seconds, it can
calculate the next state of the network before the topology changes. Traditional networks wait for a
topology change and then converge on a new vision of the world, while Starlink distributes the next
plan before anyone needs it. Therefore, convergence is replaced by the graph . The 15-second epoch
makes this execution fail-safe, and all involved components such as satellites, antennas, and
gateways migrate to the new configuration at the same time, meaning no router migrates halfway
through and no two nodes argue about which version of the map is current. If you have developed
distributed systems, you know how this works. This is epoch-based reconfiguration, so it's the same
idea applied to the physical network. So what about those changes that are truly unexpected? For
example, a satellite fails or a laser terminal breaks. Well, they are processed in the usual, boring
way. They are not predicted, they actually manifest . When the ground control center learns that the
satellite is unavailable, it simply removes it from the next optimization, and after 15 seconds the
scheduler calculates a new global task that no longer depends on the faulty device. So, essentially,
a new plan is being calculated . So far we've only talked about the connection between your terminal
and the satellite, but that's only half the story. When your data packet reaches orbit, it still has
to pass through a constellation of satellites, and the original Starlink didn't have that
capability. The first generation architecture was called “bent pipe,” and that’s apt because your
packet is first sent to a satellite, which then routes it directly to a gateway—a ground station
with large antennas and a fiber-optic connection to the terrestrial network. So , the satellite does
not forward packets through the space network, it simply returns them back to Earth. It's like a
mirror in the sky, and in the beginning it worked, but it actually created a significant limitation.
For your connection to work, you and the gateway must be in line of sight of the same satellite at

few thousand kilometers. So, if there is no gateway within this zone, your package has nowhere to go
after reaching orbit; This explains the early coverage map, as Starlink initially only appeared in

kilometers on existing fiber optic lines. But it couldn't provide reliable coverage in the middle of

gateway beneath the satellite to receive traffic. The " bent pipe" architecture made the satellite
network just a thin wireless extension of the terrestrial network. Your package spent 2 ms in space

packets had to stop returning to Earth. They were to start moving sideways, from satellite to
satellite, directly through outer space. And everything changed in 2021, when SpaceX began launching
satellites with optical inter-satellite communication lines. Instead of immediately sending each
packet to Earth, satellites could now forward traffic directly to each other. According to SpaceX's

capable of transmitting about 100 gigabits per second. Some of them reach 5,400 km, together
transporting about 40 petabytes of data every day. The engineering challenge here is obvious. The
laser communication terminal must maintain milliradian-level pointing accuracy over thousands of
kilometers while both satellites travel at orbital speed, experiencing constant altitude
perturbations. Here is the important detail that makes this possible. Starlink satellites are not
scattered around the Earth in a chaotic manner. They are organized into dozens of orbital planes,
and each such plane can be thought of as a long ring of satellites moving in exactly the same path
around the planet, one after the other. Many such rings orbit the Earth side by side, slightly
offset from each other. Each satellite's most important communications are directed to the
spacecraft immediately ahead and behind it in the same orbital plane, and they are relatively easy
to establish because these neighbors are at the same altitude, moving at the same speed along the
same trajectory. Their relative velocity is almost zero. So if you imagine the Starlink
constellation as dozens of parallel highways circling the Earth, each highway is an orbital plane, a
ring of satellites following the same path one after the other, and many such planes next to each
other form the structure of the constellation. Now imagine two cars traveling at 120 km/h on the
same road, keeping a line; It's easy to pass something between them because from each seat it seems
like the other car is standing still . The speed of 27,000 km/h is leveled off, and in practice
these intraplane connections, once established, remain active for weeks; they form the foundation of
the network, they are durable, stable, and virtually static. More complex are the connections
between neighboring orbital planes, or, to recall the analogy, other highways. These satellites do
not move in one path. They are located in neighboring rings around the Earth, and as the
constellation moves, the geometry between them is constantly changing: a satellite in a neighboring
plane slowly drifts into the desired position, and then moves away. Eventually, the laser link has
to be broken and reestablished with the next satellite that comes online . But even these shifts are
not a surprise. Orbital mechanics tell Starlink exactly when the communication between the planes
will cease to be effective and which satellite will replace it. The transfer is calculated long
before it even happens. There is one place where these laser links get significantly more
complicated. Eventually, the satellites flying north must pass those flying south, and at that point
their relative velocities do not cancel out. They actually add up, creating a convergence speed of
tens of thousands of kilometers per hour. These are the most difficult connections to establish and
maintain in the entire constellation. So, Starlink mostly avoids connecting satellites between two
opposing orbital planes, preferring routes within slower geometries whenever possible. As a result,
the network turns out to be much more structured than it seems at first glance, and some connections
are actually permanent. Others change on a predictable schedule, and the most difficult parts of the
constellation are simply avoided. Now, light in a fiber optic cable does not travel at the speed of

changes from unexpected ones . Most systems react to everything in a row, but not every change is a
surprise. Traffic patterns, scheduled maintenance, certificate expirations, and scheduled failovers
can be calculated in advance, so leave reactive systems for disruptions you typically can't predict.

protocols become necessary. The trick is to know which parts of your system fall into each category.
