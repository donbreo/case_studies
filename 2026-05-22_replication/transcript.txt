So, now I'll take you through another mental model, which is the active-active versus active-passive
replication. We will have a dedicated video just for this, but for now I just want to still cover it

the active-passive approach, and these two approaches have very different personalities, and the
choice you make has implications for cost, complexity, and recovery time. So, active-active means
that all replicas are doing real work. So, if you think about load balancers which have been
replicated, then both of these will be serving traffic. If you think about data centers that have
been replicated, then both of these data centers are accepting requests. If you think about database
nodes that have been replicated, then both of these database nodes will be taking rights, especially
if it's a multi-leader setup. So, there is no idle capacity. Everything you're paying for is
producing value. The good thing about active-active is that you're using all your hardware. The
other good thing is that failover is fast. When one replica dies, the others are already serving
traffic, so there is no warm-up period. Traffic just shifts. The hard thing is that active-active
means you have to handle the consistency problems that we have talked about, like multiple machines
making decisions in parallel, conflicts, coordination overhead, and so on. And the other approach,
which is active-passive, means one replica does all the work and the other sits idle as a standby.
The standby is fully provisioned, fully configured, fully up-to-date with replicated data, but it's
not actually serving any requests. It's just waiting. So, when the active node fails, the passive
node takes over. There is a small delay during the takeover, usually seconds, but sometimes it's
longer, during which the system is partially down, but the operational model is much simpler. Only
one node makes decisions at a time, no conflicts, no coordination overhead. Now, most production
systems use actually a mix, active-active for stateless layers like load balancers and app servers,
and active-passive for stateful layers like primary databases. The reasoning is that stateless
components don't have consistency problems, so you might as well use them all, and stateful
components have consistency problems. So, simpler is better, unless you need the extra capacity.
Now, let me tell you what replication does not fix because it is not a silver bullet. Replication
protects against random hardware failures, like a disk dying, a machine crashing, a network link
going down. For these, replication is the right answer. But, replication does not protect against
bugs that corrupt the data. If your application writes bad data to the primary, the primary
replicates it to the followers, and now all your replicas have bad data. Replication does not make
data more correct. It makes incorrect data more durable. And replication also does not protect
against operator errors. If an admin accidentally drops a table, that drop replicates to all of the
followers within seconds. Your three replica setup just lost the table three different times.
Replication also does not protect against attacks. So, if someone gets into your system, deletes
your data, replication faithfully copies the deletion everywhere. Replication also does not protect
against software bugs in the replication system itself. So, if your replication has a subtle bug
that makes followers diverge from the leader, then you will have multiple machines with different
data and no way to know which one is right. This is why backups are not replicas and replicas are
not backups. They serve different purposes. Replicas protect against hardware failures. Backups
protect against logical failures like bugs, mistakes, attacks. You need both of them. And some teams
skip backups because they think they have replicas and find out at the worst possible moment that
the replicated data is uniformly wrong. So, let me close with how senior engineers actually decide
