The downstream effects escalate and cascade. Users see stale data, payments are delayed,

the system never accepts more than it can handle. There are really three things that a system can do
when it's overwhelmed. The first is absorb. Just keep accepting work and hope you can catch up. This

catastrophically. The second is drop. When you're full, reject incoming work. This prevents the
crash, but if you drop silently without telling anyone, you create a different problem. The producer

participate. And by the way, most production systems use all three at the same time. You buffer up
to a point, you drop low priority work when the buffer fills, and you signal upstream to reduce the
incoming rate. The art is in tuning when each mechanism kicks in. So, we just learned that the

important signal, and this surprises people by the way, is not how full your queue is, it's how fast
your queue is growing. Think about it this way. A queue that's 90% full, but stable, is fine. The
system is handling the load. You're using most of your buffer, sure, but it's not growing. It's
steady, no action needed. But a queue that's only 30% full and growing fast, that's a ticking clock.
If it's growing at 5% per second, you've got about 14 seconds before it's full. That queue at 30% is
actually a bigger emergency than the queue at 90%. We'll have to instrument the growth rate, not
just the depth. If you're using Kafka, then consumer lag is your growth rate. The difference between
the latest offset and your consumer's committed offset. If that gap is widening, you're falling
behind. The second signal is response time, specifically how long your slowest requests are taking.

struggling. If everything is getting slow across the board, your whole system is saturated. The
important thing here is to not just look at averages. That's because averages hide the problems. If

which looks fine. But that one request at 5 seconds represents a real user having a terrible
experience. The third signal is worker utilization, how many of your processing threads or go

from saturation. One more spike and you've got work queuing up with no workers to handle it. And the
fourth signal, obviously, is error rate. But by the time errors are climbing, you're already in

four options for us. The first is the block option. The producer waits. It stops sending until space
opens up. This is actual back pressure at work and the full queue physically prevents the producer
from going faster. It's great for batch pipelines where waiting is acceptable. It's terrible for
user-facing APIs where blocking means the user stares at a loading spinner. Second is drop the new
item. Reject whatever just arrived. Tell the sender, "Sorry, try again later." This is the right

oldest item. Evict the oldest thing in the queue to make room for the newest. This keeps the
freshest data. Think real-time dashboards, IoT sensor readings, live market data, where showing the

reaches the source. And for back pressure to work, every layer needs to participate. Service returns
a 503 to B. Service B sees its own queue growing and returns a 429 or too many requests to service A

reduces its sending rate. And each layer passes the pressure backward until it reaches the thing

right amount of time, not too short and not too long. So, that's how back pressure propagates across

an even driven architecture, this is where you'll use these concepts every day. Kafka, as we've
already learned in this video, is pull based by design. Your consumer calls poll function and
fetches batches at its own pace, but there are specific settings that control the back pressure
behavior. And getting them wrong causes one of the most frustrating Kafka failure modes. The setting

batch of messages and takes longer than 5 minutes to process them before calling poll again, Kafka
kicks it out of the consumer group and reassigns its partitions. But rebalances are expensive. Every
consumer in the group pauses while partitions get redistributed. So, an elegant solution is consumer
pause and resume. When your processing queue is full, call consumer.pause on the partitions. The
consumer keeps sending heartbeats to Kafka, so no rebalance is required, but it stops fetching new
records. And when your processing queue drains below a threshold, call consumer.resume and the
fetching starts again. This is clean, explicit back pressure at the consumer level. And LinkedIn
runs over 7 trillion messages per day through Kafka. They've documented that using pause-resume with
lag monitoring was more effective for back pressure than any queue-based approach. And consumer lag,
which was the gap between the latest offset and your consumer's committed offset, is your primary
back pressure metric. If it's zero, you're keeping up. If it's growing, you're falling behind. If
it's growing fast, you need to either add consumers or reduce producer rate. So, monitor it and
alert on it and treat it as seriously as you would treat CPU utilization on your database server.
