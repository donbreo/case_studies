And then AWS analyzed the protocol on which all this works and decided that TCP was not the right
protocol for their data center. As you know, TCP has powered the Internet for decades, and it is
built on several key assumptions. The first assumption is that the connection takes a single route
through the network. Second, packets must be delivered in the order they are queued, and third, if a
packet is lost, you detect it and retransmit it within milliseconds. TCP was created for the
Internet, and on the Internet all three of these assumptions are correct, but in AWS data centers it
performs worse, and I will explain this using the example of the three assumptions mentioned. First:
the "clos fabric" network architecture offers thousands of parallel paths between any two nodes, but
TCP uses only one of them, since it is designed for one connection over one path, while thousands of
other available paths remain unused. Second: TCP focuses on delivery in the queue order, which means
that one delayed packet blocks all subsequent ones, even if they have already arrived. And the
third, if you remember, was about millisecond recovery, but AWS data centers measure round trip time
in microseconds, which means that recovering a lost packet takes a thousand times longer than
delivering it. There is no downside to TCP in this. TCP does its job, but the assumptions it was
built on are not very suitable for the conditions of the AWS data center. Therefore, AWS created its
own transport protocol. It's called SRD, or Scalable Reliable Datagram, and its design is published
in SIGCOMM. So you can check it out if you want. SRD takes each of the TCP assumptions and changes
it to the opposite. With SRD, packets of a single stream are simultaneously sent through 64
different paths in the network. Second: the order of packets is no longer tracked at all. Packets
arrive in random order, and the higher layer assembles them together. Third: retransmission occurs
in microseconds, not milliseconds, and congestion control is configured for exactly one network—
Amazon’s own network, since SRD does not need to operate on the open internet . The advantage of
creating your own protocol becomes apparent when something goes wrong: when a path becomes
overloaded or a switch goes down in the middle of a transmission, the flow barely notices it because
it never went only one way to begin with. A down link takes up only 1/64 of the flow's bandwidth,
and retransmitted packets are sent in microseconds over the other 63 paths. In the next section,

that has already been determined. Testing is also changing accordingly. You can create one full-size
cell in a test environment and load it to the max before real traffic arrives. You cannot load test

capacity. And one cell at full capacity is the largest unit that will ever exist in a productive
environment. But there is one tricky part, and AWS is open about it . Above the cells is a routing
layer that connects each client to its cell. This layer interacts with each client, making it the
only component that is not protected by the cell boundary. Therefore, the rule is that this layer
should be significantly simpler than anything it routes. In fact, it's just a search engine, as
lightweight and static as possible, without business logic. And this is a popular philosophy. A
single component that can affect everyone in your architecture should contain almost nothing that
can break. If you saw my video about the design of the Jira system, it's somewhat similar. Atlassian

isolate customers by dividing the service into compartments. The next technique we will look at
isolates clients without separating anything, using combinatorics. Every multi-user system has the
same worst client. The one whose request is poisonous . It may simply be bad input that is causing a
crash in whatever process is handling this request. Perhaps this is a targeted attack on their
domain. In any case, handling the traffic of this worst client kills the handler of this traffic.
Let me explain. Imagine a fleet of eight worker nodes behind a load balancer. The poison request
hits the first node, and the first node dies. The client tries again. This is what clients are made
for. And the retry goes to the second worker node. Now the second node is failing too. And after
eight attempts, one client ran his "poison" through your entire server fleet. It started with one
tenant, and now everyone is out of work . The standard protection against this is sharding. So, you
divide the eight nodes into four shards of two and hash each client to one shard. Now the "poisoned"
query kills one shard and stops. But all clients who accidentally fall on this shard fall along with
the "poisoner ". So one tenant is now disabling a quarter of your customers instead of all of them.
It's better, but still bad. Here's what the Route 53 team did instead. A Cole MacArthur Hayes
article in the developer's library details these numbers. Do not divide nodes into groups at all.
Give each client their own combination of nodes. Let's say two nodes out of eight. Now, how many
ways are there to choose two out of eight? That's 28. So, the same eight cars, but 28 possible
combinations. And each client works on one of these combinations. Now let's run through the "poison"
scenario again. The " poisoned" client disables its two nodes. Their combination is dead. But let's
look at everyone else. Almost every other client shares at most one node with the victim, while the
other remains healthy . So their retry lands on a healthy node, and they don't even notice the
failure. The only clients that went completely offline were those assigned to the same pair of
nodes, which is one of 28. So, the same eight machines, and we didn't add any new hardware. The
damage from a single problematic tenant has now dropped from 25% of customers to less than 4%,
because shards intersect and retries bypass these intersections. And here the numbers start to grow
rapidly. Take 100 nodes and give each customer a combination of five. You can choose five out of 100
in 75 million 287,520 combinations. That's over 75 million different combinations on just 100
machines. And the odds of another client sharing the same five nodes with you are 1 in 75 million.
In practice, you have more rejection domains than customers themselves. Now , if we compare the two
growth rates: the transition from eight machines to 100 is a 12.5-fold increase in equipment. But
going from 28 combinations to 75 million is an increase of about 2.7 million times. So, equipment
can only grow linearly, but insulation here can grow combinatorially. Route 53 uses this very method
to decide which DNS servers will answer queries for each client's domain. This is one of the reasons
why Route 53 offers a 100% availability SLA for DNS queries, being the only AWS service with such a
guarantee. AWS engineers have also publicly stated that this part of the service has never failed in
nearly two decades. At AWS, this idea proved so effective that it has since been applied to many of
the company's other large-scale systems and even released an open source library. So, by creating
all these combinations, they called this technique “ shuffle sharding” . This prevents one client's
failure from becoming a problem for everyone else. And now the next architectural solution solves
another problem. What happens when everything changes at the same time? Now I want to talk about a
great concept that I personally started using in the systems I design — it's called " always on" and
it became popular thanks to AWS. So I ask you to pay attention to this for the next few minutes. OK.
The obvious way to synchronize a distributed system with a large number of servers is to send only
the changes that have occurred. So if server A goes down , we send a message to the rest of the
system that server A is down. And if the server recovers, we send another update. It is effective,
but this effectiveness comes at a price. On calm days, when there are no disruptions, almost nothing
changes. Therefore, almost no work needs to be done . But on bad days, during large-scale outages or
switchovers, thousands of things change at once. Suddenly, thousands of updates need to be pushed
out across the system at the exact moment when the infrastructure is under the most strain. The more
chaotic the day, the harder the synchronization system has to work . And there is another problem.
If even one update is missed —due to a lost message, a software bug, or operator error— the various
parts of the system gradually lose synchronization. Unless something later explicitly corrects this
error, they remain inconsistent. The Route 53 health check system was designed to avoid both of
these problems. The mechanism is surprisingly simple. Every few seconds, the central service writes
the full state of each health check to a fixed-size file stored in S3. Every few seconds, each DNS
server downloads this entire file. If nothing has changed, they will still download the entire file.
And if 100,000 state checks have changed, they still download the same file in its entirety. So,
every time it's the same file, the same size, and the same amount of work. Therefore, the load never
jumps, as it does not depend on how much everything has changed. And consistency becomes almost
automatic. If one server misses an update or momentarily receives incorrect data, the next upload
fixes it with a fully correct state a few seconds later, and Cole McCarthy compares it to a coffee
machine. A barista who makes drinks to order has more work to do when more customers come in . But
the coffee machine brews the same pot whether one customer comes in or a thousand. For systems that
must remain available, AWS prefers the coffee machine method. This design is intentionally wasteful.
Most updates contain almost no new information, but that's the trade-off. A small, predictable fee
paid every few seconds in exchange for a system whose behavior does not change under load. Because
even on the busiest day, she gets almost the same amount of work done as on the quietest. This is
called “steady state operation,” and it keeps the system stable even when everything is changing at
the same time. On April 21, 2011, a routine network change in the US East region went wrong. The
traffic that EBS uses for replication was shifted to a low- bandwidth backup network, and volumes
across the region lost connectivity to their replicas. When the connection was finally restored, the
real problems began. A huge number of volumes were simultaneously trying to restart mirroring,
essentially creating a storm: they were all looking for space and sending queries to the same shared
management database that keeps track of where each volume's replicas were. And this database crashed
under load, which stopped recovery , left volumes locked, and sustained high load levels. So the
degradation lasted for several days. The postmodern lesson was structural, not procedural. They
found that a single database containing metadata for every volume in a region means that all volumes
in a region have a single point of failure. The postmodern lesson from all this was structural, not
procedural. They found out that they were using a single database that stored all the metadata. So,
a single database storing all metadata for every volume in a region means that every volume in a
region has a single point of failure. Growth only made things worse because more volumes needed to
be added each year , which meant that the next recovery event would create even more traffic to the
same shared database; and this was not a temporary bottleneck . This means that there was an
architectural limitation that became increasingly stricter as EBS evolved. The obvious solution
would be to build a bigger database: more processors, more memory, and more replicas. But AWS went
in the opposite direction and published the design as a peer-reviewed article, which they called
"millions of tiny databases." This is the title of a paper written by Mark Brooker and colleagues,
presented at the NSDI 2020 conference, which you can read online. The system they created is called
Fisalia, and the title of the article literally explains what they built: millions of tiny
databases. So, instead of one database containing metadata for each EBS volume, Fisalia runs one
database per volume. And this is a cell of seven nodes that reach agreement among themselves using
Paxos. In other words, each volume is now managed by a group of seven machines that synchronize
their state using Paxos. And if you don't know, Paxos is a consensus protocol that allows a group of
machines to agree on values, even if some of them fail. So now there are millions of volumes with
millions of independent databases across seven nodes. So when a cell fails, it only takes down the
configuration of a single volume, not the configuration of an entire region, and the most innovative
thing here is where these cells are placed . For consensus to occur, it is necessary that the
majority of its nodes are available. Therefore, Fisalia places the seven nodes of each volume
physically and topologically close to the client and replicas of that volume. There is one more note
regarding design. Fisalia is a CP system. So when a network disruption occurs, it chooses integrity
over availability, as a configuration wizard should do, and then restores availability due to its
small size and locality. So you saw that the solution to a database that couldn't scale wasn't a
bigger database. It was just millions of little bases, each placed next to whatever it controlled.
Now I want to tell you about how AWS uses mathematics and formal proofs to reduce failures. And I
think that's just great. If you think about it, any kind of testing is a form of sampling. In unit
tests, integration tests , chaos experiments, each of them tests certain parts of the system and
assumes that the untested parts behave the same as the tested ones. If you remember, at the
beginning I mentioned Vogel's phrase: everything in AWS constantly fails. So at the scale of AWS,
this also applies to probabilities. So, a bug that requires a "one in a billion" sequence of events
is no longer rare . among millions of machines performing billions of operations. A one-in-a-billion
sequence happens somewhere on a typical day, and a sample cannot capture it. There will always be
more untested parts than tested ones, and given enough scale, untested paths will work sooner or
later. So testing examines some execution paths and assumes that others behave the same way. But
mathematical proof is different. It asks whether every possible implementation satisfies the
property you are trying to guarantee. AWS uses this idea in production in two different ways. The
first is design verification. Engineers describe a distributed system using TLA+, a language for
defining how a system should behave . Model validation then examines each achievable state of that
design, looking for implementations that violate the expected behavior. This is not testing. This is
a systematic investigation of every possible path in the model. One famous example is from DynamoDB.
Model validation found a replication error whose shortest sequence of failures required 35 specific
steps. Detecting this through testing or chaos engineering would be extremely unlikely. The second
use of formal methods is much closer to what you have probably used yourself. IAM policies. An IAM
policy is not just a list of permissions. These are patterns, conditions, principles, and rules of
resources that interact in ways that are difficult to grasp manually. That's why AWS created a
system called Zelkova. Instead of testing a few example queries, Zelkova turns the policy into a
mathematical problem and asks a theorem: can any query under any conditions make this S3 bucket
public. Pay attention to the difference. It doesn't just check those requests that someone guessed
to test. It checks every possible query that may exist. This is the mechanism behind IAM Access
Analyzer, S3 Block Public Access, and even the "public" label that appears next to an S3 bucket in
the AWS console. So every time you change a bucket's policy, theorem proving evaluates whether that
policy could expose your data. They don't do this through selective requests. They prove the answer
mathematically, which I think is simply incredible. OK. In this video, we looked at seven

systems. First, small costs do not remain small when scaled. A few percent of CPU load on a single
server is nothing, but a few percent on millions of servers is entire data centers. So always
multiply the cost of one car by the number of cars you will eventually have. Second, don't build

technologies. TCP was designed for the public internet , while the AWS network worked very
differently. Therefore, AWS created a different protocol. Fifth, isolate clients where possible.
