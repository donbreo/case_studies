at a price, because when storage and compute power are combined, you inherit three problems that
never go away. And the first of them is the scaling problem. Now imagine that you need to add five
more machines to your existing 20-machine cluster that we just saw. And once you do that, the data
will no longer be evenly distributed. So you will have to redistribute terabytes of data across the
cluster before these newly added machines start to benefit the network. However, this data movement
to new machines can take days, and performance usually drops while this happens. The second is
competition for resources. Every workload uses the same cluster: your ETL pipelines, BI dashboards,
scheduled reports, and custom SQL queries all compete for the same compute pool. So one heavy load
will slow down everything else. And the third is overbooking. Engineers like you and I typically

reducing the cluster again means having to redistribute all the data. So most teams just leave it
running to avoid this headache. So these are three different problems that we've identified, and

separate compute and storage. So, if separating storage and compute solves all these problems, why
