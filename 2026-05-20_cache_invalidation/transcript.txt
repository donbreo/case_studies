about something called as the thundering herd problem. So, let's say a popular key expires. A
popular key is something that gets accessed very often, like your home page product listing or
something on your pricing page. And at the moment of expiry, a thousand concurrent requests all see
a cache miss at exactly the same time. All thousand hit the database simultaneously, and the
database, which was happily serving 50 queries per second behind the cache, gets a thousand
identical queries in 1 second, and it crashes. Amazon has publicly discussed this problem with their
product pages. Without mitigation, a popular product's cache expiry would crash their database. But,
there are three fixes for this, and these three patterns are really worth knowing. The first is a
mutex lock. If you come from Go lang background, this is very simple to understand. So, when a cache
entry expires, the first request that comes in grabs a lock and redoes and starts rebuilding the
cache. Every other request that comes in while that's happening, they just wait. And when the
rebuild finishes, the lock releases, everyone reads the fresh value, and you're done. One database
query, and everybody walks away with fresh data. And that's clean. The catch is what happens when
the rebuild is slow. If that one query takes 2 seconds, you've now got a thousand requests sitting
in a queue behind it. Your cache is fine, your database is fine, but your latency just fell off a
cliff. So, that's why the second pattern is called stale while revalidate. And this one's smarter.
When a cache entry expires, instead of blocking everyone while you rebuild, you just serve the stale
value. And meanwhile, one request in the background goes and refreshes the data. So, users get data
that's a couple of seconds old and then quietly it's fresh again. So, the database never sees a
spike and nobody waits and this is what CDNs have been doing for years. And honestly, it's what I'd
recommend for most use cases. The third one is the cleverest and it's called probabilistic early
expiration and here's the idea. Every request has a small random chance of refreshing the cache
before it actually expires and most requests do nothing. But once in a while, one of them decides to
be the refresher. And if you've got enough traffic hitting a key, someone always refreshes it just
before the TTL runs out. expiry event never really happens. No locks, no coordination, no thundering
herd. Works beautifully for hot keys and hot keys are basically keys that are often accessed from
the cache. But obviously, there's a trade-off here as well because for low traffic keys, this is
unreliable. You might go a while without any requests rolling the dice in your favor and then you're
back to a cold cache. So, three tools, three different trade-offs. Mutex is simplest but bottlenecks

falls apart when traffic is thin. Now, so far we've been talking about caching like it's just one
